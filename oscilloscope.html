Oscilloscope Playground

A dual-trace analogue oscilloscope, the kind that sat on every electronics bench in the seventies and eighties, with a cabinet of things to plug into it. Press POWER, let the tube warm up, then turn anything. Put equipment on the bench from the cabinet under the scope, then tap any socket to choose what to plug it into, or drag a cable between two. The lab notebook below has experiments that set the bench up for you.

    Lab notebook

    Each experiment sets the bench up, then leaves the knobs to you.

    1. Find the trace

      A sine wave from generator A, sweeping across the screen. Turn INTENS and FOCUS until the line is thin and bright, then try TR to tilt it.

    2. Measure a wave

      Count squares. The peaks are 2 divisions from the middle at 1 V/div, so 2 V. One cycle is 5 divisions at 0.2 ms/div: 1 ms, so 1000 cycles a second. Plug the counter in to check.

    3. Make it stand still

      The trigger is in NORM with LEVEL above the top of the wave, so nothing ever starts a sweep. Turn LEVEL down until the trace appears, then flip SLOPE.

    4. Watch the beam move

      A slow sweep and a long-glow tube. At 0.1 s/div you can follow the dot across the screen and see the wave drawn behind it.

    5. Compensate a probe

      The calibrator through a x10 probe whose trimmer is wrong, so the square wave has rounded corners. Turn probe 1's COMP until it's square. Too far and it spikes.

    6. Ripple on a power supply

      The smoothed supply looks flat at 5 V/div. Press AC and turn VOLTS/DIV to 0.5 V and the ripple appears: the capacitor sagging between top-ups.

    7. Two traces: rectifiers

      The transformer on channel I and the bridge rectifier on channel II, triggered from the mains. See how the bottom halves get flipped up.

    8. Lissajous figures

      Two generators in X-Y mode at 1 kHz and 1.5 kHz, a 2:3 ratio. Nudge generator B's frequency and the figure tumbles; land it on a whole number ratio and it freezes.

    9. What a filter does to a square

      A square wave on channel I and the same wave through the low-pass filter on channel II. Change the time constant, or move the cable to the high-pass output.

    10. Beats

      Two waves a few hertz apart, added together. They drift in and out of step, and the sum swells and fades. Plug it into the speaker to hear the wobble.

    11. Test parts without a circuit

      The component tester with a silicon diode clipped on: flat while it blocks, straight up once it conducts. Try the others in the parts drawer.

    12. Draw with sound

      The vector box in X-Y mode, drawing a spinning cube, and playing through the speaker so you can hear what it looks like.

    13. An oscilloscope clock

      The vector box drawing a clock with the real time. Look for the faint lines where the beam flicks between hands.

    14. One line of TV

      A colour bar video line, triggered on the falling edge of the sync pulse. The fuzzy bands on the bars are the colour, carried on a 4.43 MHz wave.

    15. Catch a serial message

      "Hi!" sent over a serial line. It keeps jumping because the trigger fires on any falling edge. Turn HOLD OFF up slowly until it locks onto the first one.

    16. See your voice

      The microphone on channel I. Press Microphone on the audio interface, then hum. A steady hum is almost a sine; a whistle is purer still.

    How it works

    Signals are functions of time

    Everything you can plug in is a function that gives a voltage for any moment. The scope asks for whichever moments it needs: the trigger searches forward for a crossing and narrows it down by halving, then the sweep samples the signal across the screen with a little jitter, so a wave too fast to draw fills in as a solid band, as it would on a real tube.

    The timebase is simulated, event by event

    Wait for the trigger, sweep, retrace, hold off, again. At slow sweeps that happens in real time and you watch the dot move. At fast ones there are thousands of sweeps a frame, so it samples short runs of them and scales their brightness to stand in for the rest. AC coupling, AUTO's timeout and CHOP all behave like the real circuits.

    The beam is drawn as light

    Each piece of the beam's path is a gaussian spot integrated along its length in a WebGL shader, so brightness follows how long the beam lingered. It's added into a floating-point buffer that fades at the phosphor's own rates, with a soft halo on top. That's why edges are faint, parked dots glow hot, and slow sweeps leave a tail.
